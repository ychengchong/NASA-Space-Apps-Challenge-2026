POWER Cloud and Aerosol Data Sources: Clouds, Aerosols and Trace Gases
Publisher: NASA Langley Research Center, POWER Project

Cloud properties from POWER support a range of applications:

- Solar energy: Clouds and aerosols are the two atmospheric constituents that determine how much of the top-of-atmosphere solar flux actually reaches to the surface. Both attenuate the direct beam and redistribute part of that energy into the diffuse field, so they govern not only total available irradiance but its partitioning between direct and diffuse components — the distinction that separates the resource seen by a concentrating system from that seen by a fixed-tilt array. Because their variability is the largest source of uncertainty in solar resource assessment, forecasting, and long-term yield estimation, cloud and aerosol properties are standard inputs to the irradiance models the solar community relies on. Gueymard, C.A. (2012) Sengupta et al., 2018.

- Building energy: Cloud cover influences heating and cooling loads through its effect on both shortwave and longwave radiation at the surface. Cloud information is used in building simulation tools for load calculations and for heating and cooling assessments, where the sky's radiative behavior depends on how much of it is obscured Evangelisti et al., 2019. POWER serves cloud amount and cloud optical depth from the CERES SYN1deg synoptic product, which resolves the surface radiation budget on a globally gridded, temporally complete basis Rutan et al., 2015.

- Agriculture: Crop growth responds to the light reaching the canopy, and clouds are the dominant control on how much arrives and in what form. They reduce total photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) while raising the diffuse fraction, and diffuse light penetrates deeper into a canopy than direct beam, illuminating shaded leaves that would otherwise contribute little. Clouds also govern the surface energy balance terms that drive evapotranspiration and reference crop water demand. POWER's PAR parameters are retrieved from the CERES Surface and Atmospheric Radiation Budget product, which resolves the direct and diffuse components separately Su et al., 2007, using the cloud properties derived from imager radiances by the CERES cloud algorithm Minnis et al., 2021. The agronomic significance of the diffuse fraction is well documented Mercado et al., 2009; Kanniah et al., 2012, though the net effect on productivity weakens at broader scales because total PAR falls as cloud thickness rises Zhu et al., 2019.
